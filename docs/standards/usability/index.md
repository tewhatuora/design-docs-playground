# Usability testing standards

> **Work in progress** We're working with the Health NZ Standards team on how to apply the International Organisation for Standardisation (ISO) 9241 usability standards.

These standards are for anyone testing the usability of a Health NZ product or service. To get started, use the [usability testing template](https://tewhatuora.github.io/design-docs-playground/standards/usability/usability%20testing%20template/). It puts these standards into practice for testing with Health NZ staff and health professionals. It's not suitable for testing with consumers or the public, which needs a more robust process, including information sheets, signed consent and koha.

## About usability testing

Usability testing checks whether a product or service is easy to use and meets the needs of the people who use it. The ISO definition of usability is:

*"Usability is the extent to which a system, product or service can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use (ISO 9241-11)."*

Usability is affected by:

- **the users:** who they are
- **their goals:** what they're trying to do
- **their situation:** where and how they use it ("context of use")

We can measure usability by:

| Question | Measure | What good looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can people complete their tasks? | Effectiveness | Most people complete tasks |
| How easy is it? | Efficiency | Most people rate it easy |
| Are people satisfied with it? | Satisfaction | Most people are satisfied with their experience |

## Scope of these standards

Usability testing is a validation method. It works in the **solution space**, checking whether a design works for the people who will use it. These standards don't cover discovery research, which works in the **problem space**: understanding the problem and people's needs before deciding what to build.

Templates for research, recruitment, consent, scheduling and reimbursement are in the [research templates](https://hauoraaotearoa.sharepoint.com/:f:/r/sites/1001713/Design%20artefacts/01_Templates/Research%20templates?d=wc42a8c91a82b4961bd84d0104967d7d4&csf=1&web=) on Digital Health Strategy and Design SharePoint.

### Ways to run usability testing

These standards focus on **testing with a facilitator** (moderated testing), where someone runs each session with a real user. They also work for **unmoderated testing**, where participants test on their own, but you'll need a specialist online tool. Ask the Digital Health Strategy and Design team which tools are approved.

We're still working out how **testing with synthetic users** (artificial intelligence acting as users) fits in. It can help check your test plan, but it's no substitute for real New Zealand users.

## The minimum

1. Decide what you want to learn and what good looks like.
2. Test with real users using realistic scenarios.
3. Share findings and agree what you'll fix.

## The standards

The standards are grouped into three stages, matching the template:

1. [Plan](#1-plan)
2. [Run the test](#2-run-the-test)
3. [Report results and act on feedback](#3-report-results-and-act-on-feedback)

### 1. Plan

| # | Standard | Level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1.1 | Review the goals of the programme or project and the problem it's solving. | MUST |
| 1.2 | Review any available research, such as analytics, feedback, discovery findings, surveys or previous usability testing. | MUST |
| 1.3 | Test significant changes, new functionality and areas of known usability risk. Set a date you need findings by, early enough to change the design before release. | SHOULD |
| 1.4 | Write down what you want to learn: a few questions you want answered. For example, "Can staff find and update a roster change without help?" | MUST |
| 1.5 | Test with real users, not just the project team. Describe them by job title, where they work and how they use the product. | MUST |
| 1.6 | Include a mix of people (one person can cover several): new users, experienced users, people less confident with computers, people with disabilities or impairments (such as vision, hearing, mobility or cognitive), and Māori and Pacific participants. | SHOULD |
| 1.7 | Test with 3 to 5 people in each round. | SHOULD |
| 1.8 | Plan a separate walkthrough with managers or subject matter experts. Their views add to testing with real users but don't replace it. A subject matter expert who uses the product can take part as a real user. | COULD |
| 1.9 | Describe where people use it, for example at a desk, on the ward, on a laptop or on a phone. | SHOULD |
| 1.10 | If it replaces a system or process, note what people use now, for example one or more old systems, a spreadsheet or a paper form. | SHOULD |
| 1.11 | Offer help with the technology for online sessions, such as joining the call or sharing a screen. | COULD |
| 1.12 | Decide how long the session will be and stick to it. About 45-60 minutes is a good starting point. | COULD |
| 1.13 | List the tasks to test. Choose tasks that will help answer your questions (1.4). | MUST |
| 1.14 | Turn each task into a scenario: a real situation that doesn't give away the answer. Describe the situation, not the steps, and don't use on-screen labels. Include any details people need, like dates or names. For example, instead of "Click Request leave", try "You'd like to take Friday 20 December off as annual leave. Request the day off." | MUST |
| 1.15 | Decide what success looks like for each task, for example: "most people complete it without help". | MUST |
| 1.16 | Test the same tasks on the current system first, to get a baseline. | COULD |
| 1.17 | Never use real patient or staff information. Prepare realistic fictional or test data, such as test logins and fictional records, before the session. | MUST |
| 1.18 | Do a practice run with a colleague, to check the prototype works and the scenarios make sense. | COULD |
| 1.19 | Have a colleague take notes, so you can focus on the participant. If you're on your own, record the session and take notes afterwards. | COULD |
| 1.20 | Agree as a team what you'll ask consent for: what you'll record, who will see it, where you'll store it, how long you'll keep it, and whether their name goes in an engagement catalogue. | MUST |

### 2. Run the test

These standards are in the order you'll run them in each session. The template's test script covers them all.

| # | Standard | Level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2.1 | Welcome the participant. Explain that you're testing the product, not them, and that there are no right or wrong answers. Ask them to think out loud as they go, and tell them they can stop or take a break at any time. | MUST |
| 2.2 | Get each participant's informed consent before you start recording, using what the team agreed in 1.20. Recording is optional. Record their consent in writing or on the recording. | MUST |
| 2.3 | If you have an engagement catalogue, ask for consent to add the participant's name | COULD |
| 2.4 | Start with a few warm-up questions to help the participant relax and give you context, such as their role, how long they've been in it, what a typical day/week looks like. You can also ask about their experience with the product (new vs experienced) and how often they use it (first time or rarely, sometimes, or most days). Don't spend so long that the session becomes discovery research. | SHOULD |
| 2.5 | Ask people to do each task without help. Only step in if they're stuck and can't move on. Record whether they completed it (pass), completed it with help (pass with help) or didn't complete it (fail). | MUST |
| 2.6 | After a task, ask "Was that easy, OK or hard?" and "Why?" This is useful for critical tasks, as finishing a task doesn't always mean it was easy. | COULD |
| 2.7 | Record the time and number of clicks for each task, and any wrong turns or retries. You can count these afterwards from the recording. | COULD |
| 2.8 | At the end, ask "Overall, how was [product] to use? Easy, OK or hard?" and "Why?" | COULD |
| 2.9 | If the product replaces something, ask "Compared with what you use now, is it better, the same or worse?" and "Why?" | COULD |
| 2.10 | Wrap up. Ask if there's anything they'd like to add and thank them for their time. | SHOULD |

### 3. Report results and act on feedback

Use the template's one-page findings summary to report results and record what you'll do.

| # | Standard | Level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3.1 | Record how many people you tested with and their roles. | MUST |
| 3.2 | Check your results against each task's success criteria (1.15) and record whether each one was met. Report counts, for example "2 of 3 passed", because the numbers are small. | MUST |
| 3.3 | Record what you saw and what people said, in their own words. Include ratings only if you used them. Keep participants' names out of the findings summary. | SHOULD |
| 3.4 | For each issue, note how many people it affected and rate its severity as high, medium or low. | COULD |
| 3.5 | Note what's working, not just what went wrong. | COULD |
| 3.6 | Review findings with the project team and agree what you'll do about each issue. | MUST |
| 3.7 | For issues you won't fix, record why, including any known risks. | SHOULD |
| 3.8 | Share findings with the project team, and with the change and training team for anything that affects training, support material or rollout. | SHOULD |
| 3.9 | Share the findings with the people you tested with. | COULD |
