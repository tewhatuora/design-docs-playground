# Usability testing standards

> **Work in progress** We're working with the Health NZ Standards team on how to apply the International Organisation for Standardisation (ISO) 9241 usability standards.
>
> **Coming soon** Ready-to-use templates for guerrilla testing: quick, informal testing.

These standards are for anyone testing the usability of a Health NZ product or service.

## About usability testing

Usability testing checks whether a product or service is easy to use and meets the needs of the people who use it. The ISO definition of usability is:

*"Usability is the extent to which a system, product or service can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use (ISO 9241-11)."*

Usability is affected by:

- **the users:** who they are
- **their goals:** what they're trying to do
- **their situation:** where and how they use it ("context of use")

We can measure usability by:

| Question | Measure | What good looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can people complete their tasks? | Effectiveness | Most people (80% or more) complete tasks |
| How easy is it? | Efficiency | Most people rate it easy |
| Are people satisfied with it? | Satisfaction | Most people are satisfied with their experience |

## Scope of these standards

Usability testing is a validation method. It works in the **solution space**, checking whether a design works for the people who will use it. These standards don't cover discovery research, which works in the **problem space**: understanding the problem and people's needs before deciding what to build.

Templates research, recruitment, consent, scheduling and reimbursement are in the [research templates on DHSD SharePoint](https://hauoraaotearoa.sharepoint.com/:f:/r/sites/1001713/Design%20artefacts/01_Templates/Research%20templates?d=wc42a8c91a82b4961bd84d0104967d7d4&csf=1&web=).

### Ways to run usability testing

These standards focus on **testing with a facilitator** (moderated testing), where someone runs each session with a real user. They also work for **unmoderated testing**, where participants test on their own, but you'll need a specialist online tool. Ask the Digital Health Strategy & Design team which tools are approved.

We're still working out how **testing with synthetic users** (artificial intelligence acting as users) fits in. It can help check your test plan, but it's no substitute for real New Zealand users.

## The minimum

1. Decide what you want to learn and what good looks like.
2. Test with real users using realistic scenarios (tasks).
3. Share findings and recommendations.

### 1. Decide what to test

| #   | Standard | Level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1.0 | Review any available research, such as analytics, feedback, discovery findings, surveys or previous usability testing, to help identify what to test and what success looks like. | MUST |
| 1.1 | Identify who will use it (the real users). Use specific role names, job titles or user groups where possible. | MUST |
| 1.2 | Describe where they'll use it, for example in the office, remotely, in front of consumers, on a laptop. | SHOULD |
| 1.3 | List the tasks to test based on the goals of the usability testing. Prioritise tasks that help answer the key questions you want to learn about. | MUST |
| 1.4 | If it replaces a system or process, note what people use now, for example one or more old systems, a spreadsheet or a paper form. | SHOULD |
| 1.5 | Define success criteria for the tasks, and for any ratings you use. For example, at least 80% of participants complete top tasks without help. | MUST |
| 1.6 | Plan usability testing for significant changes, new functionality or areas of known usability risk. Do it early enough for findings to be acted on before implementation or release. | MUST |
| 1.7 | Plan a separate walkthrough with managers and/or subject matter experts. Their views add to testing with real users; they don't replace it. A subject matter expert who uses the product can take part as a real user. | SHOULD |

### 2. Set up the test

| #   | Standard | Level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2.1 | Recruit real users, not just the project team. | MUST |
| 2.2 | Recruit a mix of participants (one person can cover several of these):<br>• new users<br>• experienced users<br>• people less confident with computers<br>• people with disabilities or impairments, such as vision, hearing, mobility or cognitive<br>• Māori and Pacific participants | SHOULD |
| 2.3 | Recruit at least 5 participants for each round of testing. | SHOULD |
| 2.4 | Turn each task from 1.3 into a real situation or scenario that doesn't give away the answer. Describe the situation, not the steps, and don't use on-screen labels. For example:<br><br>**Add a case note**<br>Instead of: "Click Add case note"<br>Try: "You've just spoken to someone on the phone about their case. Record what you discussed."<br><br>**Find a case**<br>Instead of: "Use the search bar to find case 12345"<br>Try: "Someone calls and gives you their name. Find their details."<br><br>**Escalate a high-risk case**<br>Instead of: "Click the Escalate button"<br>Try: "You're worried someone on your caseload is at risk. Let your team leader know." | MUST |
| 2.5 | If you can, test the same tasks on the current system first to get a baseline. | SHOULD |
| 2.6 | Never use real patient or personal information. Use realistic fictional or test data where needed. | MUST |

### 3. Run the test (your test script)

This stage is your test script. The standards below are in the order you'll run them in each session: consent, warm-up, tasks, ratings and wrap-up. Your tasks come from 1.3, written as scenarios in 2.4. Your script also needs each task's success criteria (1.5), and test data only (2.6).

**To save time,** you can do consent (3.1) and the warm-up questions (3.2) before the session, for example by email or a short online form.

| #   | Standard | Level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3.1 | Get each participant's informed consent before the session. Explain that recording is optional, their name won't be in reports, and they can stop at any time. Record their consent in writing or on the recording. | MUST |
| 3.2 | Start with a few warm-up questions to help the participant relax and give you context. Gather enough context about the participant's role, experience and use of the product or service, but don't spend so long that the session becomes discovery research. For example:<br>• "What's your role or job title?"<br>• "How long have you been working in [role]?"<br>• "Can you describe a typical day in your work?"<br><br>**Experience:** "How would you describe your experience with [the product or service]?"<br>1. New to it<br>2. Still learning<br>3. Can do the usual tasks, but sometimes need help<br>4. Confident without help<br>5. Expert, and help or train others<br><br>**Frequency:** "How often do you use [the product or service]?"<br>1. Less than once a month, or never<br>2. About once a month<br>3. About once a week<br>4. About once a day<br>5. Several times a day | SHOULD |
| 3.3 | Ask people to do each task without help. Only step in if they're stuck and can't move on. Record whether they:<br>• completed it (pass)<br>• completed it with help (pass with help)<br>• didn't complete it (fail)<br><br>If a failure was caused by the test itself, such as a broken test environment or wrong test data, don't count it. Fix the problem and rerun the task, with the same participant or another one. | MUST |
| 3.4 | Record the time and number of clicks each person takes to finish each task, and note any wrong turns or retries, for example going to the wrong screen or starting again. You don't need to do this during the session. If you record it, you can count afterward. Before testing, time an experienced user doing each task and count their clicks, so you can compare. | SHOULD |
| 3.5 | For tasks you want to know more about, such as critical tasks, ask the ease question straight after. Finishing a task doesn't always mean it was easy.<br><br>**Ease:** "How easy or difficult was [that task]?"<br>1. Very difficult<br>2. Difficult<br>3. Neither easy nor difficult<br>4. Easy<br>5. Very easy | SHOULD |
| 3.6 | If a rating needs explanation or would help the team understand the result, ask "Why did you give that rating?" | SHOULD |
| 3.7 | At the end of the test, ask the overall ease question. Keep the question and scale as written, so results can be compared.<br><br>**Overall ease:** "Overall, how easy or difficult was [the product or service] to use?"<br>1. Very difficult<br>2. Difficult<br>3. Neither easy nor difficult<br>4. Easy<br>5. Very easy | SHOULD |
| 3.8 | Ask the satisfaction question. Keep the question and scale as written.<br><br>**Satisfaction:** "Overall, how satisfied are you with your experience?"<br>1. Very dissatisfied<br>2. Dissatisfied<br>3. Neither satisfied nor dissatisfied<br>4. Satisfied<br>5. Very satisfied | SHOULD |
| 3.9 | If the product replaces something, ask the comparison question. Keep the question and scale as written.<br><br>**Comparison:** "Compared with [what you used before], how would you rate this?"<br>1. Much worse<br>2. Worse<br>3. About the same<br>4. Better<br>5. Much better | SHOULD |
| 3.10 | Wrap up: thank them for their time, ask if they have anything else to add, and tell them what happens next with their feedback. | SHOULD |

### 4. Report results

> **More guidance coming soon**, including a results template you can copy.

| #   | Standard | Level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4.1 | Check your results against your success criteria (1.5). For each task and rating, say whether it met the criteria. Report counts alongside percentages, for example 4 of 5 (80%), because the numbers are small. | SHOULD |
| 4.2 | Make sure all feedback is recorded and available to act on, including people's reasons in their own words (3.6). Share it with the project team, and with the change and training team for anything that affects training or rollout. | SHOULD |
| 4.3 | Report the results for task completion, ease ratings, satisfaction ratings, comparison ratings (if used), and participant comments and observations. | SHOULD |
| 4.4 | Report the participant mix, including role types and any experience or frequency ratings collected in 3.2. | SHOULD |

### 5. Act on feedback

| #   | Standard | Level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5.1 | Review findings with the project team and agree what will be fixed. | MUST |
| 5.2 | Record decisions for issues that won't be fixed, including any known risks. | SHOULD |
| 5.3 | Share findings that affect training, support material or rollout activities with the appropriate teams. | SHOULD |
